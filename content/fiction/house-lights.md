“It is eight minutes past ten, Sanjay. The opening slide is still up, the investors’ preview is in two hours, and someone from the board just texted me asking if we have a tech failure. What is going on down there?”

Ambi’s voice came through the phone crisp and controlled, the way it always did from the fourteenth floor. I could hear his pen tapping against his desk.

“There’s no tech glitch,” I said. I was in the glass sound booth at the side of Auditorium 2. “The mics work. The deck is loaded. AJ just isn’t going out there.”

The tapping stopped.

“What do you mean he's not going out there? Where is he?”

“In the room behind the stage.”

Ambi sighed. “Look, I can’t come down. I have the CFO calling in ten minutes. Tell me plainly what is happening. Both sides. Don’t edit it for me.”

I leaned against the sound console.

“Well, people started coming in at 9:30. Leadership in the first two rows, the delivery unit behind them. Vikram from Finance sent the margin outlook and guidance before they arrived.”

“I asked him to. People shouldn’t be blindsided by the numbers.”

“Yes. You should have seen them checking their phones. Two hundred people in the first wave of the restructuring, sitting with their notebooks shut. They’re still looking at the blue slide that says *Operating Model 2.0*.”

“Is AJ pushing back on the numbers? Because I sat through four steering committee reviews with him. He designed this restructuring.”

“I know.”

“He built the pilot platform. Thirty-eight percent less time on test-case generation. Forty-two percent faster ticket triage. He showed us those results himself. He championed this -  he saw this coming eighteen months before our competitors did. Why is he freezing now?”

“At nine-fifty, he asked Kris to walk onto the stage with him.”

“Before the doors closed?”

“Everyone was seated. The house lights were down for the projection. From where he stood on the podium, he couldn't see past the front row. He asked to bring the lights up.”

“Why?”

“He wanted to see who he was talking to.”

Through the booth window, I watched two engineers in the seventh row whisper to each other. One checked his watch.

“Did Legal sign off on the revised packages?” Ambi asked. “If he’s worried about severance, we went thirty percent above market. We aren’t throwing people onto the street.”

“He hasn’t mentioned severance.”

“Then remind him what we’re dealing with. Our revenue per employee is sixty-four thousand dollars. Our two closest rivals are guiding toward eighty-five next year. If we keep the staffing model we have, we lose four major renewals by Q4. That puts four thousand people at risk. He knows the math, hell, he built this whole thing.”

“He isn’t arguing with the math.”

“Then what is he arguing with?”

I turned towards the other window. It looked into the passage beside the stage. Through the open green-room door, I could see AJ sitting in a black mesh chair, elbows on his knees. Kris stood near the doorway with his hands in his pockets.

“I think i know what this might be - he saw Raghavan in the fourth row,” I said. “On the aisle.”

“The banking architect?”

“Yes. He taught AJ to read a production trace. AJ was twenty-four, terrified of breaking a live deployment. Raghavan’s been here eighteen years now. Now the migration platform handles seventy percent of his legacy assessment work, and his role is on the sunset list.”

“We offered him an advisory transition.”

“Six months to train the system that replaces his team. He’s fifty-two, Ambi. He knows what that emails meant”

“Meera is two rows behind him.”

“I know Meera too.”

“She gave AJ his first delivery pod. Nobody else would hand him, a 29 year old opinionated senior engineer a thirty-person account. She covered for his mistakes with the client for two quarters.”

“She was right about him.”

“Her layer is being cut by forty percent. She’s sitting there smiling at the person next to her.”

Ambi exhaled into the phone.

“Sanjay, I’ve spent twenty-eight years in this company. Some of those people stayed with us through 2008. Through the pandemic. What do you want me to do? Go to the board at noon and say we’re keeping the old structure because Meera was kind to AJ in 2016?”

“You asked me what happened. I'm just telling you.”

“Go on.”

“He kept looking across the rows. Karthik’s on the far right, the Java lead from his old pod. He invited AJ to his housewarming last month. A flat in Sarjapur. Twenty-year home loan.”

Ambi said nothing.

“And Ananya’s three seats down. She joined six months ago. Still brings a spiral notebook to standups to take notes. She’s in the junior intake we’ve decided we don’t need.”

In the front row, Vikram turned towards the booth and tapped his smartwatch. 

“AJ stood there for maybe forty seconds,” I said. “He’d stopped in the middle of the mic check. Then he walked offstage and sat down in the side room. Kris followed him. I went in to see if he was sick.”

“Was he?”

“His hands were shaking. He tried drinking some water. He put the clicker down on the table like it weighed twenty kilos and looked at Kris, and told him he couldnt do it.”

“Just like that?”

“He asked Kris to tell him the economics were wrong.”

“Were they?”

“That’s what he wanted. An error. Something he’d missed.”

I could still see the bottle beside AJ’s chair. He had left the cap off.

“He said, ‘Give me an out. Tell me the models don’t work. Tell me the clients aren’t asking for rate cuts.’ Kris let him talk.”

“And?”

“Then he said, ‘That’s the part that’s tearing my head apart, Kris. I can’t even pretend this is evil.’ He kept looking at Kris, waiting for him to object.”

“AJ said he’d built the demo himself. He knew what it could do. I know the productivity gains are real. I know if we don't modernise, the market will chew this company up in three years. Hell, I even believe that five years from now, this technology will create jobs we haven't even named yet. But, but. We keep talking like the machine is just clearing away drudgery. Sometimes it is. And sometimes it’s sawing off the top and bottom rungs of the ladder, and in five years we’re all going to stand around acting shocked that nobody knows how to be a senior architect anymore.”

Ambi’s chair creaked over the line.

“Then he agrees with us. That is the town hall.” Ambi only heard what he wanted to hear - blind to the other parts. 

“He pointed out at the auditorium. ‘I believe in the new operating model,’ he said. ‘But What do I tell Raghavan today?’”

“He should tell him about the transition package.”

“That isn’t what he meant.”

“I know what he meant, Sanjay.”

For a moment, neither of us spoke. I could hear Ambi's phone notifications going off.

“AJ said he’d learnt everything from those people,” I went on. “How to build software that work, how to lead a team. How to keep a client’s trust after something went wrong. And now he has to explain why we can do without them.”

“He said, ‘I didn’t learn any of that from a white paper, Kris. Raghavan sat beside me when I didn’t know what I was doing. Meera put her name against mine when nobody else would. I owe them. And now my job is to walk up to a microphone and explain, using slides I helped design, why the skills they spent fifteen years mastering are now a margin problem.’”

He went quiet for a bit. 

“When I became CEO, I had to retire the man who hired me,” Ambi said. “Head of infrastructure. Brought me in as a project lead in 1998. I didn’t sleep for a week.”

“Did you tell AJ that?”

“No.”

The pen tapped once. Then twice. 

“You treat people as well as you can, and you do the job. Someone has to make the decision.”

“He has made it. He helped you make it. That’s what he can’t get past.”

I looked at the open slide on the big screen.

“He asked Kris, ‘What happens when doing what you believe is necessary also means betraying the people who made you capable of doing it?’ I didn’t know what to say to him either.”

Through the doorway, I saw AJ press his palms against his forehead. Kris had not moved.

“Then he started talking about Ananya,” I said. “About how we keep saying people will be free to do higher-order thinking.”

“They will.”

“He asked how Raghavan learnt to do it. Five years debugging other people’s code. The work we’re taking away from the freshers. He said, ‘Who teaches Ananya now? We’re cutting her job and getting rid of the person who could teach her.’”

“That is an operational design question. We’ll redesign the apprenticeship model.”

“He’s looking at the people who’d have to do that.”

“We will need a different way to train them. Nobody is saying we stop developing talent. But our competitors  are now quoting twenty-two percent lesser. We have to keep up.”

“He knows.”

“You keep saying that.”

“Because he does. He said if it were just about beating a competitor, he’d be onstage already. You’ve seen him fire people. You’ve seen him walk away from accounts.”

“Yes.”

“He said these people did what we asked. Learnt the stacks. Worked the weekends. Karthik bought that flat last month and invited him to the housewarming.”

I stopped. Ambi was waiting for me to finish.

“He kept coming back to that.”

“Buying a flat can’t be a guarantee of a job, Sanjay.”

“He knows that too. He said, ‘I’ve fired underperformers. I’ve sat across from angry clients. I could do that. But these people haven’t failed at anything. They did what we told them would make them valuable. Now I’m supposed to stand there and tell them the rules have changed. They’ll be looking at me, Kris. I used to sit in the cubicle next to them.’”

Out in the hall, someone opened a notebook, looked at the page, and closed it again.

“Before I came back to the booth, he said he didn’t want any of this anymore. The stocks, the industry panels. He told Kris he’d rather resign than read Slide 6 out loud.”

“Resign?”

“That’s what he said.”

“He said, ‘I don’t want the credit for leading a transformation if this is what I have to do to earn it. Give it to someone else. I’d rather walk out the door today than go back on that stage and call this progress.’”

Ambi didn’t mention the CFO. I waited for him to ask me to put AJ on the phone.

“Where’s the clicker now?” he asked instead.

“On the table. He slid it away from him. Told me to send Vikram out, or postpone the whole thing.”

“AJ said he couldn’t find a single honest sentence left in the deck.”

“He wrote it.”

“I know.”

“Vikram can take them through the numbers.”

“AJ thought of that too. Then he looked at the clicker and said, ‘Listen to me. They still lose their jobs. Vikram gets through the slides, and I get to leave believing I refused.’ He rubbed his face and told Kris, ‘I don’t know what I’m asking for anymore.’”

Ambi said nothing.

The murmur in the hall had grown louder. Vikram stood and walked towards the side door, his phone pressed to his ear. Kris stepped into the passage and held up a hand. Vikram stopped. For a moment I thought he would push past, but Kris shook his head, and Vikram went back to his seat.

“It is sixteen minutes past ten, Sanjay.” Ambi’s voice was tight again. “I cannot leave two hundred people sitting there indefinitely. Either we cancel this town hall and trigger an absolute panic across every floor, or AJ walks back out there. What is happening right now? Is he moving?”

I looked into the side room. AJ hadn't picked up the clicker. He was still sitting there, shoulders dropped, completely emptied out. No longer the star who had seen the future, just a man caught between what the situation demanded and what his conscience could bear.

Across from him, Kris finally pulled a chair over, turned it around, and sat down.

“No,” I said. “Not yet.”

“Then what are we waiting for?”

Kris leaned forward. AJ looked up at him.

“Kris just sat down,” I said. “He’s starting to talk.”
